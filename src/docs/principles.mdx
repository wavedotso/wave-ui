# Principles

Wave UI favors restraint. When two approaches are equally correct, we pick the one that
adds the least — fewer states, fewer cursors, fewer special cases. A signal earns its
place before it ships, so components read as one surface rather than a pile of parts.

## The cursor speaks in a binary

A pointer means _clickable_; the default arrow means _not_ — that's the whole vocabulary.
Disabled controls fade and stop responding (`pointer-events-none`); we
don't add `cursor-not-allowed` on top. It's partly redundant — the opacity already says
"disabled" — but mostly it simply can't work: `pointer-events-none` takes the element out
of hit-testing, which suppresses the cursor entirely, so a `cursor-not-allowed` set beside
it never paints. It's one or the other, and blocking the interaction is the stronger choice.

## Focus survives high-contrast mode

Components suppress the default browser outline with `outline-hidden`, never `outline-none`.
`outline-none` deletes the outline outright; `outline-hidden` keeps a real outline that's
merely _transparent_. That distinction matters in Windows High Contrast / forced-colors
mode, where our custom focus ring — a `box-shadow` — is stripped away entirely: the
still-present outline gets repainted in a system color, so keyboard focus stays obvious.
`outline-none` would leave those users with no focus indicator at all. Hide the outline,
never delete it.

## Structure reacts to direct children, not grandchildren

Layout-shaping selectors use the direct-child combinator — `has-[>[data-align=block-end]]:…`,
not `has-[[data-align=block-end]]:…`. The `>` scopes the match to a component's **own** slots:
an addon a consumer drops _directly_ into an InputGroup can flip it to a column, but a
`data-align` that happens to sit deep inside some nested component never should. Drop the `>`
and a grandchild can silently reshape its ancestor.

State is the deliberate exception. Focus and invalid selectors _omit_ the `>`
(`has-[[data-slot=input-group-control]:focus-visible]:…`) because the real control is often
rendered a level down via Base UI's `render`-prop composition, so the group must catch the
state anywhere inside it. The rule of thumb: **structure keys off direct children; state keys
off any descendant.**

## A field's messages sit below it, error first

A field's description and its error both go **below** the control, in that order: the error
right under the field it's about, the description beneath it. The error is the most urgent
thing on the field, so it sits closest; the hint stays visible, because the moment something
goes wrong is exactly when a reader needs it. We don't swap one for the other, and we don't put
the hint above the input — one place, one order, in every field and every form.

The error carries the state on its own: the control gets the danger ring and the message
says what's wrong, while the label stays neutral rather than turning red.

## Solid colour for action, neutral for information

Solid colour is for what asks to be acted on or noticed now: buttons, badges, unread counts
and critical states take their palette's 500 with white text, the same in every theme.

Everything that informs stays neutral. Sections, callouts, alerts and tags keep the theme's own
background and text, and carry their colour in the icon and the edge: a stronger border, or the
signature gradient.

Information stays calm and readable, holds up on cheap screens and in sunlight, and never
competes with people's own content. Colour only reinforces the meaning; the icon and the words
carry it, so it reads the same for everyone, whatever colours they see.

## Opacity comes in four steps

Translucency is picked from a short scale, each step tied to a job, never tuned by eye per
component:

- **10** — faint tints: `bg-contrast-inverse/10`, a key cap on a tooltip
- **30** — invalid and disabled: `ring-danger/30`, `opacity-30`
- **50** — focus halos: `ring-focus/50`
- **80** — a filled control's hover and the glass fill: `hover:bg-primary/80`, `bg-elevated/80`

A value outside the scale is a question to answer, not a tweak to keep: if 10 reads too faint
and 30 too strong, the role is usually wrong, not the number. Fewer values also means the same
job looks the same everywhere, which is most of what makes a set of components feel like one.
The one standing exception is 90, the fill that glass falls back to where the browser can't
blur (below).

## Lines divide and bound

Wave draws the lines between and around things with the theme's own foreground made
translucent, at three strengths, one job each:

- **`line`** — 5% of the foreground: what divides. Separators, the dividers between rows and
  sections, hairlines.
- **`edge`** — 10%: what bounds a surface. The edge around a card, a dialog, a menu or a toast.
- **`contour`** — 15%: what outlines a control or a region. The border of an outline button, a
  badge or a toggle, the slider thumb, a menubar, a layout region.

All three are borders, part of the thing they belong to.

## Rings are halos

A ring is drawn outside a thing, like a halo around it, so Wave keeps rings for the states that
sit around a control: focus (`ring-focus/50`, and `ring-danger/50` on a red control) and invalid
(`ring-danger/30`). A surface's edge is never a ring. Drawn outside, it lies past a menu's glass,
on the page behind it: where it crosses text it flickers between the letters and the page, on in
some places and gone in others. Windows' high contrast mode also drops every ring, which would
leave a menu with no edge at all. A border is part of the surface: it sits on the glass, stays
even, and high contrast keeps it.

## Focus survives high contrast

Windows' high contrast mode drops every ring, the focus halo included, and repaints the page in
a few system colours, so a highlight fill disappears too. Something else has to show where
focus is, and that's the browser's outline. Tailwind's `outline-hidden` is a rule with two
halves: normally it hides the outline, so the halo can show focus instead; in high contrast it
draws one. The variant in front of it decides which element the rule is switched on for, and
it must be the one that shows focus or the highlight, never every element:

- **`focus-visible:outline-hidden`** on anything whose halo is a `focus-visible:` ring: buttons,
  fields, checkboxes, tabs, cards that are links. Also on what can take focus without a halo of
  its own: popups, dialogs, tab panels.
- **`focus:outline-hidden`** on menu items, whose highlight follows focus, even under the
  pointer.
- **`data-highlighted:outline-hidden`** on list options (Select, Combobox, Autocomplete). In a
  combobox the text field keeps focus and the options are only highlighted, so this is the
  only way high contrast can show which one is.
- **`data-active:outline-hidden`** on the active one-time-code slot.

Plain `outline-hidden` is never used: switched on for every element, its high contrast half
outlines all of them, so every item is boxed and the focused one looks like the rest. An element
that never takes focus, such as a popup's positioner, needs none. In normal mode none of this
changes anything you can see.

## Fills have one job each

Wave fills things with the theme's own foreground made translucent, one job each:

- **`highlighted`** — 10% of the foreground: where attention lands. The hovered row, the item
  picked with the arrow keys, a trigger whose menu is open.
- **`neutral`** — 15%: what gives a control its body, its look before anything is chosen.
  The fill of fields, tracks and unchecked boxes, a scrollbar thumb. (Tailwind's own
  `neutral-50` … `neutral-950` greys are a separate palette Wave doesn't use.)

All of these are translucent, so each sits the same distance from whatever is beneath it — the
page, a card, a menu or glass — and a theme only has to supply its foreground. They step from faint to
strong, so a hovered row always reads lighter than the empty field beside it. A colour is named
for its job, not for where it lands: a separator drawn as a 1px fill is still `bg-line`. When a
boundary has to be intense — a layout region, a control's outline — it takes `contour`.

## Hover points, primary chooses

Every item, row and button takes its background from one of three recipes, so a highlight
means the same thing wherever it appears:

- **Hover and highlight** — `bg-highlighted`, on anything without a fill of its own: menu and
  list items, ghost and outline buttons, toggles, sidebar items, table rows, items rendered as
  links. In a menu or a list, the row under the pointer and the row picked with the arrow keys
  are one state, so they look the same.
- **Hover on a filled control** — the fill fades to 80 (`hover:bg-primary/80`) with the same
  backdrop blur as glass, so a button and a menu over the same picture read as one material.
  Where the browser can't blur, it fades only to 90.
- **Selected, current or on** — `bg-primary` with white text: the active tab, the sidebar's
  current page, a pressed toggle, a selected row, a checked control. Primary means _chosen_,
  never merely _pointed at_, which is why hover never fills with it: a hovered row that looks
  like the current page makes the current page impossible to find.

Focus stays separate and sits on top of any of these, as the focus halo (`ring-focus/50`).

A few things show hover another way, on purpose. Form fields already carry the neutral fill, so
hover reveals their border (`hover:border-focus`). Inactive tabs and breadcrumb links brighten
their text, and the accordion trigger underlines. A surface that is a link steps up a level
instead: a card lifts to the elevated surface, a muted item to the full secondary fill. Small
fills that never sit over a picture, such as link badges and the native file button, fade to
80 without the blur.

## Floating surfaces are glass

A menu floats over the page as glass: the elevated surface at 80 with a 24px backdrop blur
(`backdrop-blur-xl backdrop-saturate-150`), so what's beneath shows as a soft glow of colour
rather than shapes you could read. On a plain page it simply looks solid, and that's fine.
Where the browser can't blur, the fill holds at 90 (`not-supports-backdrop-filter:` carries
the fallback), so text never sits over a sharp, busy background.

The blur goes on the surface itself, never on a `::before` layer: a layer blurs only part of
what's behind it.
